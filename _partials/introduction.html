Beyond GDP

There is an international debate about framing climate policy beyond GDP — judging its consequences for people’s lives, not only for economic output. DigiÉire brings this perspective to flood adaptation in Ireland, placing wellbeing at the centre of how risks and responses are assessed.

The global evidence is stark. In 2024, global mean temperature reached 1.53°C above the 1850–1900 baseline, making it the warmest year in the instrumental record. Fossil CO2 emissions continued to rise, reaching 38.6 billion tonnes that year. Thermal expansion and the loss of glaciers and ice sheets raised global mean sea level by about 218 mm between 1900 and 2020. Recorded flood disasters worldwide increased from around 26 a year in the 1970s to 171 a year across 2015–2024, although improved reporting accounts for part of that increase.

Natural variability interacts with this long-term warming. El Niño redistributes heat and rainfall across the Pacific every few years, influencing weather patterns beyond the region. It amplifies or moderates the underlying warming signal rather than creating it: the strong events of 1997–98 and 2015–16 stand out against a temperature trend that was already rising.

Ireland’s position in the North Atlantic places it under the direct influence of ocean circulation and weather systems that redistribute heat and moisture around the planet. Its extensive coastline and settlements along estuaries make rising seas a particular threat, connecting changes in the global ocean and the loss of distant glaciers and ice sheets to the wellbeing of communities at home.

These global changes have local consequences. Ireland is small enough to model as a whole, with towns and cities built along estuaries that already flood. As sea levels rise, flooding can become more frequent in places people have lived for generations.

The costs extend beyond damage to buildings and infrastructure. The worry before a storm, the months in temporary accommodation, the insurance that will not renew, and the sense of being unheard by the bodies meant to help can leave lasting effects on people’s lives. These consequences are difficult to capture through economic losses alone. They are central to the wellbeing perspective that DigiÉire brings to flood adaptation.

What should we adapt for, if not for how well people are able to live?

  • Coastal communities first — where exposure is highest
  • To 2100 — the horizon adaptation decisions actually reach
  • Wellbeing as the quantity maximised, not damage avoided
